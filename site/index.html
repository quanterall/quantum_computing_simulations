Founded in Varna in 2003, Quanterall now works on quantum algorithms, quantum error correction and quantum circuit simulation. What exists: ten quantum circuits you can run on this page, simulated on the Erlang virtual machine, with their source code public; a simulation engine we built over the last two years for the EU research project TechUPGRADE, which we now use for simulations in other fields, quantum computing among them; and an Erlang practice that goes back to 2009.

What exists, what is simplified,
what is missing.

Exists
  • Live circuits on QxSim, our state-vector simulator in Elixir, cross-checked against Qiskit (which code runs what).
  • Five codes with full correction cycles; the surface code under gate-level noise on QxStab, our stabilizer simulator, checked against Stim.
  • Reproductions of known results, with data: repetition sweeps, a threshold study, benchmarks, photonic schemes (HOM, the KLM gates).
Simplified
  • Shor's modular multiplication is applied as one permutation unitary, not decomposed into gates; N up to 21.
  • State-vector codes: one error per run, noiseless gates; the surface code there uses one reused ancilla.
Missing
  • A positive research result: our first hypothesis — a BEAM decoder with bounded tail latency near compiled speed — was falsified; a batched design is next.
  • An independent review of the physics, and a paper.
  • The public repository and a citable release.
Next
  • Our research question: the first answer is no; next, batches per process and a dedicated machine.
  • The repository with a DOI; a first preprint.
All the details
  • Circuits (QxSim). Bell pair; teleportation of a random state, undone on Bob's side so that his qubit reads 0 every shot; Grover's search on 2 to 10 qubits with its success probability per iteration against theory; a variational classifier; Shor's algorithm in two forms: compiled for N = 15, a = 7 — 7⁴ ≡ 1, so the period is built into the circuit; it shows the structure, not a speedup — and general, for N = 15 or 21 and any a: 2n counting qubits, controlled multiplication by a^(2^j) mod N (a permutation unitary), the inverse QFT, continued fractions; with a = 2 it factors 15 in about 75 % of shots and 21 in about 84 %, and a = 14 (for 15) or 5 (for 21) never, as theory says. Each outcome is named: factors, no period found, period odd, a^(r/2) ≡ −1; an a sharing a factor with N returns it without the circuit; the exact distribution from the state vector stands beside the samples. The modular multiplication is a permutation of basis states computed classically — an oracle, not a gate-level arithmetic circuit — so no gate counts or resource estimates follow from it. With 2n counting and n work qubits Shor needs 3n qubits: within QxSim's 19, N < 64; the public endpoint stops at 21 to keep runs short. Grover's oracle and diffusion are gates — H, X and a multi-controlled Z — with the multi-controlled Z applied as one gate, not decomposed. Every run repeats exactly with its seed.
  • Codes on the state vector. Bit-flip (corrected by a majority vote of the readout), phase-flip, Shor-9, Steane-7 and the distance-3 surface code (all eight stabilizers measured one after another on one reused ancilla: 10 qubits, a lookup decoder) each correct every single-qubit error. One error per run; gates and measurements without noise.
  • Gate-level noise (QxStab). The surface code with its 17 qubits (d = 3) up to 97 (d = 7), d rounds, noise on every gate, reset and measurement, decoded by PyMatching on Stim's detector error model.
  • Limits. QxSim stops at 19 qubits in its 1 GB container and is about 100 times slower than Qiskit Aer there; QxStab is 8 to 30 times slower than Stim run on as many cores.
  • Process models. Entanglement as linked processes (CHSH S measured live with its error); teleportation between two BEAM nodes.

How this was built. Most of the code since 28 October, and the checks around it, were written with an AI coding assistant (Claude, by Anthropic), directed by our team; that is why so much is dated within two days, after a project begun in February 2026. We rely on independent tools rather than on the assistant: the circuits are compared with Qiskit, the stabilizer sampler with Stim, the decoding is PyMatching's, and the scripts and data are published. No independent physicist has reviewed the work yet; that review belongs to the preprint.

Which code runs what

QxSim checked against Qiskit: 55 random circuits on 2 to 12 qubits (2,310 gates: H, X, Rx, Ry, Rz, CNOT, CZ, controlled phase, swap), each final state vector compared with Qiskit's — the worst fidelity is 1 − 7 × 10⁻¹⁵, while one angle changed by 0.01 rad shows as 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ (data, tools/beam_runs.exs xval). Circuits with measurement in the middle: every error-correcting code with every single error, before and after correction, and teleportation of random states, rebuilt in Qiskit as dynamic circuits — mid-circuit measurement, reset, corrections conditioned on the measured bits — and run on Aer, 4,000 shots each, each comparison with its own seeds. 8 comparisons have one outcome on both sides: all equal. For the other 61, chi-square tests: the smallest p-value is 0.03 and they spread over the whole range, with more near 1 than a uniform spread would give (the test is conservative on sparse tables; the comparisons are not all independent). Each check is shown to bite: Aer driving Bob's X and Z with the wrong bits, Aer's Steane decoder off by one position, a Qiskit circuit with one angle changed by 0.01 rad — each is caught at once (data, tools/dynamic_xval.py).

Why now
In 2024 a surface-code quantum memory was run below the error-correction threshold: its logical error rate fell by a factor of about 2.1 with each step up in code distance (from 3 to 5 to 7) (Google Quantum AI, Nature 638, 920; 2025). Fault-tolerant computation at a useful scale has not been demonstrated yet, and practical advantage on real problems is still argued over. That is the gap where simulation is useful: codes, decoders and noise can be studied before the hardware exists.

Where our past work carries over: we already test systems by injecting faults, and supervision trees and parallel Monte Carlo are how the BEAM runs many noisy shots with each failure isolated. The BEAM suits offline, parallel simulation and the orchestration around it; it is soft real-time, so it is not a decoder for real hardware, which needs microsecond latency from FPGAs or ASICs. Our track record in simulation is the two years building TechUPGRADE's simulation engine.

Where it does not: a quantum state cannot be copied (no-cloning), so redundancy cannot mean replication; and errors are found indirectly, by measuring syndromes without reading the data. Those are the parts we are learning.

Quantum Algorithms

A pure-Elixir state-vector simulator (QxSim) inside our demo service, with no simulator library underneath. Every run is an Erlang process, isolated from the others, and runs go in parallel. // Exploratory tracks — speculative, and labelled as such
X-01 · Entanglement as linked processes

We model an entangled pair as two Erlang processes whose states are linked: measuring one fixes what the other will show. This is a classical simulation: a third process holds the shared state centrally and both sides ask it, so the model reproduces the statistics of entanglement, not nonlocality. The demo below measures S live and shows it with its standard error (about ±0.06 over 2000 rounds), against 2 for any classical model and 2√2 ≈ 2.83 in theory. For teaching: it makes visible that the correlation comes from the shared state, not from a message between the two.

X-02 · Teleportation as process migration

We model teleportation as a process that migrates between two BEAM nodes: the state leaves one node and is rebuilt on the other, carried only by a Bell pair shared in advance and two classical bits sent over the network — as in the protocol, where nothing quantum travels after the pair is made. The simulator knows ψ — it draws it at random and checks the result — so the demo shows the protocol's structure, not a physical transmission. For teaching and for prototyping protocols.

Why we publish the speculative work: a lab that only shows finished results is telling you nothing about how it thinks.

// Reproduction · repetition-code sweeps

Logical against physical error rate,
with the data.

Noise model. A bit-flip repetition code of distance d keeps logical |0⟩ on d data qubits, with d − 1 ancillas measuring neighbouring parities. Each data qubit flips (X) independently with probability p; gates and syndrome measurements are perfect; a minimum-weight decoder picks the correction.

Exact, and what it checks. There are only 2ᵈ error patterns. Each is run once through the syndrome circuit on our NumPy state-vector engine (the TechUPGRADE engine's quantum module; tools/repetition_sweep.py calls it, and computes the closed form beside it), and the logical error rate is the sum of the failing patterns' probabilities. For this noise model a closed form exists — the binomial tail Σk>d/2 C(d,k) pᵏ(1−p)ᵈ⁻ᵏ — and the two agree to 6 × 10⁻¹⁶. That agreement is close to guaranteed: bit flips on |0…0⟩ keep the state a single basis state, so it tests the circuit's logic, not superposition or phase. The test of those is the cross-check against Qiskit below. A Monte Carlo experiment with 100,000 shots per point (seed 20261029) is drawn beside it.

What it does not show. Circuit-level noise — errors in the gates and in the syndrome measurement itself — has no simple formula, and is where real study begins. That, and codes beyond the roughly 20 qubits a state vector allows, need a stabilizer simulator; both are on our list (see Where we are).

// Reproduction · gate-level noise, on a stabilizer simulator

The surface code under circuit noise,
checked against Stim.

The simulator. QxStab, a stabilizer simulator written in Elixir: a tableau (Aaronson–Gottesman) runs each circuit once without noise for a reference, then a Pauli-frame sampler runs the shots, bit-packed — 1,024 shots in one integer per qubit — in parallel batches, one BEAM process each. It is the method Stim uses, written for the BEAM.

The experiment. Rotated surface codes of distance 3, 5 and 7 (17, 49 and 97 qubits) keep a logical |0⟩ for d rounds of stabilizer measurements. Noise at rate p everywhere: depolarizing after every gate (one- and two-qubit), bit flips after resets and before measurements, depolarizing on the data at each round.

Validation, and what it covers. Each circuit is written out in Stim's format: Stim accepts every detector as deterministic and finds the code distance d for each (our first CNOT order gave only (d+1)/2, a hook-error mistake Stim caught). The detector error model, and so the decoding, comes from Stim; what is compared is the sampling alone. Sampled by Stim and by us, both decoded by PyMatching: the logical error rates agree. Stricter, with exact binomial tests (our count against Stim's, given their sum): every detector's firing rate at all 21 points (3,360 tests, 0.30 % below p = 0.0027 against 0.27 % expected); every detector together with the logical observable, the correlation that sets the logical error rate (3,360 tests, 0.36 %); every pair of detectors for d = 5 at p = 0.6 % (7,140 tests, 0.18 %); and the decoded logical error rates themselves (21 tests, the smallest p-value 0.70 after Bonferroni correction). The detectors are not independent, so these shares are a guide, not a calibrated test. The tests have power: Stim at p = 0.65 % against Stim at 0.6 % is flagged on 97 % of detectors (numbers, tools/stim_exact.py).

What it shows. The curves cross between p = 0.6 % and 0.8 % — three distances and seven error rates bracket the crossing, they do not estimate it (that would need finer steps near it and a finite-size fit). The bracket is in line with published values for this noise model, roughly 0.5 to 1 %: below it, a larger code protects better. The decoder is PyMatching's, not ours.

// Reproduction · photonic schemes from the literature

Photons on a beamsplitter, the KLM gates,
fusion, detectors and loss.

What this is. Simulations of schemes from the literature — not photonic hardware. A photon simulator in Fock space, written twice from one conventions file (docs/optics_conventions.md): QxFock in Elixir (this service) and fock.py in the TechUPGRADE engine. Each applies an interferometer two ways (expanding creation operators; permanents by Ryser's formula). On 60 random interferometers of 4–8 modes and 1–4 photons, every output amplitude agrees: the two routes to 1e-15, Python with Perceval (Quandela) to 2e-16, Elixir with Python to 2 × 10⁻¹⁵; one beamsplitter sign flipped shows as 0.61.

Hong–Ou–Mandel. Two photons meet on a balanced beamsplitter; when they overlap fully they always leave together. With exponential pulses offset by τ (overlap e^(−γ|τ|/2)) the coincidence probability is ½(1 − e^(−γ|τ|)): simulated at 81 delays, it matches to 2e-16 and is exactly 0 at τ = 0.

The KLM gates (Knill, Laflamme, Milburn, Nature 409, 46, 2001). The nonlinear sign gate, from KLM's own 3-mode unitary and from the beamsplitter form (angles 22.5°, 65.5302°, −22.5° and a π phase): on 50 random inputs α₀|0⟩ + α₁|1⟩ + α₂|2⟩ it gives α₀|0⟩ + α₁|1⟩ − α₂|2⟩ with probability 1/4 and fidelity 1 − 10⁻¹⁵. Two of them make a CZ on dual-rail qubits (8 modes, 4 photons): process fidelity 1 − 3 × 10⁻¹⁶, success exactly 1/16.

Checks that bite, and one that did not. The middle angle off by 0.5° lowers the CZ fidelity to 0.9997. Leaving out the π phase was expected to break the gate; it does not — it gives diag(1, −1, −1) per NS gate, and the circuit becomes CZ followed by Z on both qubits, still a CZ up to single-qubit phases. So that is no power check, and we say so.

Fusion (Browne and Rudolph, PRL 95, 010501, 2005). Type-I fusion, simulated photon by photon — polarising beamsplitter, 45° rotation, one polarisation-resolving detector — acts on the two input qubits as (|0⟩⟨00| ± |1⟩⟨11|)/√2 (to 10⁻¹⁶), succeeds with the input's weight on |HH⟩ and |VV⟩ (½ for cluster qubits), and its failures are Z measurements of both. Type II succeeds with probability ½ and projects onto one Bell state, Φ⁺ or Ψ⁺: linear optics tells apart two of the four. On state vectors, fusing the ends of linear clusters of n and m qubits (2–5) gives the cluster of n + m − 1 with fidelity 1 − 2 × 10⁻¹⁶.

Growing a chain. A long linear cluster grows by fusing k-qubit pieces onto its end, each piece itself built from Bell pairs by fusion; on a failure the chain loses its end qubit and the piece its first. Bell pairs used per qubit gained (Monte Carlo, 20 × 20,000 attempts):

Bell pairs alone (k = 2) never grow the chain — success adds one qubit, failure takes one away. Three-qubit pieces cost 8 pairs per qubit, or 7 when the two-qubit leftover of a failure is reused: the review's "2 × 4 − 1 = 7". Its "6.5" we do not find in any strategy we simulated; the best are 7.0, with three- or four-qubit pieces and reuse (data, fusion checks, /quantum/optics/fusion).

Measurement-based computation (on state vectors). One-bit teleportation through a two-node cluster and a rotation along a chain (adaptive angles, Pauli byproducts) reach fidelity 1 on random inputs. The review's four-node example does not hold as drawn: its Fig. 10 chain (CZ₁₂CZ₂₃CZ₃₄) gives a CZ between the teleported input and the fourth qubit — CNOT-class by its local invariants, but needing gates before as well as after; the couplings its Eq. 56 writes (1–2, 2–3, 2–4) do give a CNOT for every outcome and for superposition inputs, with Pauli byproducts when built from CZ gates (data).

// The research question

Everything above reproduces known results.
This is the question we want to answer.

Question. Under concurrent load, does a union-find decoder on the BEAM — every syndrome its own lightweight process, many decoded at once on all cores — keep its tail latency bounded, at a throughput near a compiled decoder's?

Baselines, to separate the algorithm from the language. Union-find in a compiled language (Rust or C++), and in plain Python; and PyMatching 2.4 (minimum-weight matching, C++) — each run under the same load as ours: a worker pool on the same cores, fed from a queue, with 1, 100 and 10,000 syndromes in flight. Same machine, same syndromes (sampled by QxStab and by Stim from the threshold-study circuits, d = 3 to 9, p = 0.1 to 1 %). The decoding graph comes from Stim's detector error model for every decoder, ours included: there our work depends on Stim.

The deadline, and why the BEAM. The BEAM is soft real-time: it will not decode for superconducting hardware, whose rounds take about a microsecond. Trapped-ion and neutral-atom machines run rounds of a millisecond or more, and the software that orchestrates any machine works in milliseconds; we test against a 1 ms deadline at d = 5. The question is whether preemptive per-process scheduling keeps the tail there when the queue grows, where a single-threaded decoder's queue waits pile up.

Hypothesis, and what falsifies it. At d = 5: our 99th-percentile latency stays under 1 ms with 10,000 syndromes in flight, growing no more than 2× from 1 to 10,000; and our throughput per core is within 2× of compiled union-find. Either number missing falsifies it. We expect to lose against PyMatching, which decodes such syndromes in microseconds, and we report by how much — and the logical error rate, since union-find is a little less accurate than matching. A partial result still says something: bounded latency at a lower throughput would describe what the BEAM is for.

Protocol. One warm-up pass; 10 repeated runs per point, medians with 95 % intervals by bootstrap; garbage collection left on and reported (BEAM processes collect their own heaps; we record the time spent); scripts, raw timings and syndromes published with the results.

Result · 29 October 2026 · the hypothesis is falsified

First, our design had a flaw. With a fixed number of syndromes in flight, Little's law makes the mean latency that number divided by the throughput: 10,000 in flight at 1 ms needs 10 million syndromes a second from any decoder. That measures throughput, not scheduling. We kept the closed-loop runs, and added the usual test: syndromes arriving at random (Poisson) at 50, 80 and 95 % of what each decoder sustains through its own harness, and the latency from arrival to answer.

The setup. The same 8 fast cores (of a 20-core Arm machine that also runs other services) for every decoder; the same graph and syndromes at p = 0.4 %. What it does show. A process per syndrome costs the BEAM more than the decoding: its harness alone, with no decoding, has a 99th percentile of 2.41 ms at half load for d = 3 — spawning and messaging at a few hundred thousand a second is the bottleneck, not the scheduler's fairness. C++'s tails are bimodal across seeds — microseconds in some runs, milliseconds in others — which fits interference from the machine's other work on shared cores; a clean tail measurement needs a machine to itself. Python's harness costs more than any deadline here (its no-op p99 is tens of milliseconds), so for PyMatching and Python union-find only throughput is meaningful: about 124k and 93k syndromes a second through it at d = 5.

// The substrate

The science of
superposition

A register of n qubits is described by 2n complex amplitudes. A measurement returns one outcome, with probability given by its amplitude squared; the amplitudes themselves are never read out. Speedups come from interference: an algorithm arranges the amplitudes so that wrong answers cancel and right ones reinforce before that one measurement.

Superposition — n qubits, 2n amplitudes; a measurement returns one n-bit outcome. The demos' histograms are many such outcomes.
Entanglement — correlated states that cannot be described independently: a resource for quantum communication and, in many algorithms, for speedups. See it in the Bell pair.
Interference — amplitudes cancel wrong paths and reinforce right ones. Many quantum algorithms work this way. See it in the Grover circuit.

|ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩,  |α|² + |β|² = 1

// Process models on the BEAM

Entanglement as linked processes. Teleportation as a process that migrates.

Two quantum concepts modelled with what the Erlang virtual machine does best — isolated processes and nodes — for teaching and for demonstrating the concepts. Both run live below, on real BEAM processes; the numbers are measured, not drawn.

X-01 · Entanglement

Each round spawns three processes: a pair process holding the shared state |Φ⁺⟩, and Alice and Bob. They never message each other; each asks the pair to measure its own qubit at an angle. Whoever asks first collapses the shared state, and the other reads a correlated result. A classical simulation: the pair process is a central state holder, so this shows the statistics of entanglement, not nonlocality.

The physical system: two photons with entangled polarization

A crystal makes the photon pair; neither photon has a polarization of its own until one meets a polarizer. A polarizer turns half as much as the qubit's measurement axis, so the settings are 0° or 45° for Alice and 22.5° or 67.5° for Bob, as in the Bell tests of Aspect (1982) and Weihs (1998).
